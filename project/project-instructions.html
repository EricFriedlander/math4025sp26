Group Project: Predicting Physical Properties of Yellowballs

Math 4025 — Statistical Machine Learning, Spring 2026

Introduction

“Yellowballs” are compact, roughly circular structures in the Milky Way first identified by citizen scientists participating in the Milky Way Project, an online image classification platform where citizen scientists classify images from the Spitzer Space Telescope. The telescopse uses something called photometry to measure their brightness at different wavelengths. Yellowballs are candidate sites of intermediate- to high-mass star formation — regions where gas and dust are being heated and compressed by newly forming stars and appear “yellow” in color due to the wavelengths they emit most strongly at.

What is photometry? Photometry is the measurement of how bright an astronomical object appears at specific wavelengths of light. For this project, you should think of different wave lengths of light as your features, where the value associated with each wavelength is called a flux density. These measurements are obtained from a telescope equipped with different filters (or detector channels) and record a flux density at each wavelength specified by the filter, producing a set of brightness measurements across the electromagnetic spectrum. Together, these measurements form a spectral energy distribution (SED), a kind of fingerprint that (hopefully) encodes information about the object’s physical properties (e.g., temperature, mass, luminosity, and physical size). Rather than observing each object individually with expensive follow-up instruments, photometry gives astronomers a fast, large-scale way to characterize thousands of objects simultaneously.

The MIRION catalog (Milky Way Infrared ONset; Wolf-Chase et al. 2025) contains 6,176 Yellowballs, with physical properties derived for 3,945 of them using multi-wavelength photometry from Spitzer and Herschel space telescopes.

The scientific question: Estimating the physical properties from the photometric signature alone is both a time-intensive process and relies on a wider range of photometric measurements than is available in the MIRION catalog. So our research questions are:

Can we predict a physical property of a Yellowball from its infrared photometric signature in the MIRION catalog? If so, which wavelengths are most important for making accurate predictions, and what does that tell us about the underlying physics of these objects?

Accurate photometric predictions would allow astronomers to characterize newly discovered objects without the time-intensive follow-up observations needed to derive physical properties directly.

Your Assignment

Each group is assigned one physical property of Yellowballs to predict. See Canvas for your group’s assigned target variable.

The table below describes all eight target variables. Each of these variables represents a physical property of the Yellowballs in our data. Three of these variables (DIAM, MASS, L_BOL) are what we call distance-dependent and will be much harder to predict because measurement of their physical properties rely on the distance to the Yellowball. groups assigned those targets will need to incorporate the distance data file into their analysis.

Target Description Units Distance-dependent?
DIAM Physical diameter of the Yellowball bubble pc Yes
MASS Total gas and dust mass enclosed \(M_\odot\) Yes
L_BOL Bolometric (total) luminosity of the central source \(L_\odot\) Yes
LM Luminosity-to-mass ratio (evolutionary stage indicator) \(L_\odot/M_\odot\) No
TEMP Dust temperature from a greybody SED fit K No
LRATIO Ratio of warm-to-cold luminosity (dimensionless) No
T_BOL Bolometric temperature (SED-weighted mean temperature) K No
SURF_DENS Surface mass density g/cm² No

Recommended Reading

These papers provide scientific context for the data and the astrophysical questions your analysis addresses. You are not expected to understand every technical detail, but reading the introductions and results sections will help you interpret your findings meaningfully.

  • Wolf-Chase et al. (2025) — The MIRION catalog paper: describes the Yellowball sample, the photometry, and how the physical properties were derived. https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4357/ae31f6

  • Anderson et al. (2012) — Background on multi-wavelength infrared methods for characterizing star-forming regions. https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2012/01/aa17640-11.pdf

  • Elia et al. (2017) —The Hi-GAL compact source catalogue – describes process for extracting physical properties from Herschel photometry, which is relevant for understanding the target variables. https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article-pdf/doi/10.1093/mnras/stx1357/19343235/stx1357.pdf

Data

Your GitHub Classroom repository contains a data/ folder with eight files. Start with MIRION_meta_all_1.csv to locate your target variable, then join it with MIRION_spec_1.csv to get the photometric features. See the Data Wrangling Tips guide for detailed loading instructions.

The following provides a summary of each of the data sets provided to you. Note that you are not required (and not expected) to use all of them.

MIRION_meta_all_1.csv — Target Variables

  • Format: CSV,
  • Rows: 3,945
  • Columns: YB (you can use this to join it to other datasets) and all eight target variables: DIAM, MASS, L_BOL, LM, TEMP, LRATIO, T_BOL, SURF_DENS.

MIRION_spec_1.csv — Long-Wavelength Photometry

  • Format: CSV (in transposed format)
  • Rows: Provides four wavelength bands (8, 12, 24, 70 \(\mu\)m) originally from Spitzer Telescope (see MRT-phot.txt for original), six additional flux bands (160, 250, 350, 500, 870, 1100) from the Herschel, and YB IDs that you can use to match this to other datasets. Note that the four wavelengths from the Spitzer telescope are considered more important. See the Data Wrangling Tips for how to load and reshape this file.

MRT-phot.txt — Spitzer Photometry (primary feature table)

This dataset contains the original Spitzer photometry for all 6,176 Yellowballs, along with quality flags and other metadata. You may find it useful to use the uncertainties and source flags in your analysis.

  • Format: Fixed-width format
  • 38-line header
  • Rows: 6,176
  • Key columns:
Column Description Units
YB Yellowball source ID
GLON, GLAT Galactic longitude and latitude deg
MWPR Milky Way Project radius deg
F8, F12, F24, F70 Flux densities at 8, 12, 24, 70 µm Jy
e_F8, e_F12, e_F24, e_F70 Flux uncertainties Jy
N8, N12, N24, N70 Number of photometric measurements per band
f_SAT Saturation flag (4-char string, one digit per band)
f_MULTI Multiple-source flag
f_NOSRC No clear source detected (4-char string, one digit per band)
f_PCONF Poor photometric confidence (4-char string, one digit per band)
f_CEXT Highly circular extended source flag

Other datasets

The following are additional datasets that you may find useful. Note that more details explanations of their variables are provided in the files themselves, near the top.

MRT-dist.txt — Distances

  • Format: Fixed-width format
  • Rows: 6,176
  • Key columns: YB, DIST (adopted distance in kpc), e_DIST. Required for groups predicting DIAM, MASS, or L_BOL.

MRT-velo.txt — Radial Velocities

  • Format: Fixed-width format
  • Rows: 6,176
  • Key columns: YB, VEL (LSR radial velocity in km/s), e_VEL. Optional additional feature for groups wanting to go further.

MRT-xmat.txt — Catalog Cross-Matches

  • Format: Fixed-width format
  • Rows: 6,176
  • Key columns: YB, ID_HIGAL, ID_ATLASGAL, ID_CORNISH, ID_RMS, ID_WISE. Cross-match identifiers and classification types from Hi-GAL, ATLASGAL, CORNISH, RMS, and WISE catalogs. These are categorical identifiers; they require encoding to be used as features.

MRT-hcsc.txt — Herschel-Derived Properties

  • Format: Fixed-width format
  • Rows: ~3,945
  • Key columns: YB, L_HERS, e_L_HERS, T_HERS, e_T_HERS. Herschel-based physical properties with uncertainty columns, overlapping with the MIRION targets below.

Deliverables

All deliverables are due April 30. Submit via your GitHub Classroom repository (link on Canvas) and the Canvas assignment page.

1. Presentation (7 minutes)

A non-technical slide presentation for an audience of astronomy researchers — scientists who understand star formation but are not necessarily familiar with machine learning methods. Your goal is to explain what you did, what you found, and what it means for our understanding of Yellowballs.

  • Strictly 7 minutes; plan for 2-3 minutes of Q&A afterward
  • All group members must participate in presenting
  • Slides should be visually clear with well-labeled figures
  • Explain your ML methods in plain language; focus on what the model does, not the math
  • Interpret your results in astrophysical terms

2. Executive Summary (1-2 pages)

A one-page document summarizing your findings for the same astronomy-researcher audience. This is not a technical report.

  • State the prediction problem and your approach in 2-3 sentences
  • Highlight your key result (model performance + what drives predictions)
  • Include 1-2 strong data visualizations
  • Is shorter than 2 pages, including figures

3. GitHub Classroom Repository

Your repository must contain all code needed to reproduce your analysis, written as .qmd files that render without errors. See the Quarto + Python Guide.

  • All analysis in .qmd or .py files (no Jupyter notebooks)
  • README.md completed with group info, target variable, and reproduction instructions
  • GroupPlanningWorksheet.qmd rendered to HTML and committed
  • Code is organized and readable

4. Team Contribution Report (submitted individually on Canvas)

A short paragraph (3-5 sentences) per team member describing what tasks you personally completed, approximately how many hours you contributed, and any challenges you encountered.

Getting Started

  1. Accept the GitHub Classroom assignment (link on Canvas) and clone the repository to your local machine.

  2. Read the Data Wrangling Tips. Load MIRION_meta_all_1.csv and MIRION_spec_1.csv, join them on YB, and make sure you can access your target variable.

  3. Explore your target variable. Plot its distribution. What does it look like?

  4. Explore your features. Plot the flux distributions. Look at scatterplots of each flux band vs. your target. Check for missing values using missingno.

  5. Build a baseline model. Start simple: linear regression using the four Spitzer flux bands (F8, F12, F24, F70) as features, evaluated with cross-validation. This gives you a benchmark.

  6. Iterate. Add features (flux ratios, long-wavelength bands, quality flag indicators), try different models, tune hyperparameters. Each change should be motivated by something you observed in your EDA or in the previous model’s behavior.

  7. Evaluate your final model with cross-validation. Report \(R^2\) and RMSE. Interpret what the model does and doesn’t predict well in astrophysical terms.
